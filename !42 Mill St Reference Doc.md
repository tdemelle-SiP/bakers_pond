## Massachusetts Wetlands Filing Process

| # | Form / Abbrev. | Result | Stage |
|---|-------|---------------|--------|
| 1 | **RDA** - Request for Determination of Applicability (WPA Form 1) | Conservation Commission issues **DOA** (Determination). Negative DOA = clear to proceed; Positive DOA → file NOI. | **Pre-screen (optional)** |
| 2 | **ANRAD** - Abbreviated Notice of Resource Area Delineation (WPA Form 4A) | Commission issues **ORAD** confirming wetland boundaries for 3 years. | **Resource-boundary confirmation (optional)** |
| 3 | **NOI** - Notice of Intent (WPA Form 3) | MassDEP assigns the seven-digit **DEP File #** (e.g., 338-0706). | **Permit application** |
| 4 | — | Commission reviews plans; 21-day abutter comment window. | **Public hearing** |
| 5 | **OOC** - Order of Conditions (WPA Form 5) or Denial | OOC recorded at Registry of Deeds before work starts. | **Decision** |
| 6 | — | Follow OOC conditions; request amendments/extensions if needed. | **Work period** |
| 7 | **RCOC** - Request for Certificate of Compliance (WPA Form 8A) | Commission issues **COC**; record it to lift lien. | **Close-out** |

## Appeals and Variations
|  | **SOC** - Superseding Order of Conditions if OOC is appealed to MassDEP.  
|  | **Amended OOC** - for approved plan modifications.  
|  | **Extension Permit** - extends an unexpired OOC (WPA Form 7).  
|  | **Enforcement Order** - issued for unpermitted or non-compliant activity (WPA Form 9).

## Contesting an ORAD or SOC
Where the community has influence and visibility into the process

#### ORAD (Order of Resource Area Delineation)
- **0-10 days after issuance** - File a **Superseding ORAD** request with MassDEP (310 CMR 10.05(7)(a)).
- **>10 days, within 3-year validity** - Ask the local Commission for an **Amended ORAD** (no appeal if denied), or submit a **new ANRAD/NOI** to re-open the boundary.

#### SOC (Superseding Order of Conditions)
- **0-10 days after issuance** - File a **Request for Adjudicatory Hearing** with MassDEP's Office of Appeals & Dispute Resolution (OADR).
- **Within 30 days of OADR final decision** - Seek **judicial review** in Superior Court (M.G.L. c.30A §14).
- **Any time before the SOC expires (~3 yrs)** - Request an **Amended SOC** from MassDEP (Policy 85-4) or file a **new NOI**. Violations can trigger an **Enforcement Order**.

From the search results and legal framework I found, there is a clear distinction between state Wetlands Protection Act authority and local bylaw authority. The Parkview Electronics Trust case shows that local commissions can act under local bylaws even when DEP finds no state jurisdiction. But that's different from what happened here.

In this case:

The Commission denied the ANRAD
DEP upheld that denial on appeal
Then through settlement, they gave an ORAD anyway but "under bylaw only"

This is different from Parkview where there were conflicting determinations between state and local. Here, both state and local INITIALLY agreed (both denied). Then the local reversed itself through settlement.

I can't definitively say it's unprecedented without reviewing all Massachusetts wetlands settlements, but it does appear to be highly unusual and problematic.

Typically, when DEP upholds a local denial, that reinforces the local decision - both levels of review agreed the wetlands were more extensive than claimed. The normal "bylaw only" scenarios occur when:

DEP finds no state jurisdiction but the local commission finds local jurisdiction (like in Parkview)
Local bylaws explicitly protect resources not covered by state law

But here, you have both authorities initially agreeing (denial at both levels), then the local commission reversing itself through settlement to grant something the state explicitly rejected. This creates a weird precedent where:

Scientific determinations can be negotiated away if you threaten to sue
Local commissions can essentially override DEP denials by claiming "bylaw only" authority
The appeal process becomes meaningless - why appeal to DEP if you can just threaten litigation locally?

The danger is that future applicants could argue: "The Commission has previously granted boundary delineations through settlement even after DEP upheld denials, as long as it's under local authority only."

Whether this is truly "unprecedented" would require comprehensive research, but it certainly appears to be an abuse of the dual regulatory system. The bylaw-only designation seems like a fig leaf to avoid acknowledging they're granting what both authorities already determined was scientifically unjustified.

It's procedural laundering - taking a denied application and washing it through settlement negotiations until it comes out clean on the other side.
